📖 Textual & Lexical Analysis Template

A textual analysis project examines language use, discourse patterns, and terminology in mathematical contexts using quantitative and qualitative techniques.
← Back to All Project Types

Project Overview

This type of research treats text as data. The goal is to uncover patterns in how language is used to communicate mathematical ideas. It can reveal hidden assumptions, track the evolution of terminology, or analyze how students and instructors talk and write about math.

Common Methodologies:

  • Quantitative Lexical Analysis: Analyzing word frequency, co-occurrence, or sentiment in a collection of texts (a "corpus"). For example, tracking how often certain proof-related words appear in textbooks over time.
  • Corpus Linguistics: A broader approach that studies language patterns in large, structured corpora of text, such as collections of student writing or research papers.
  • Discourse Analysis: A qualitative approach that explores how language is used to build mathematical meaning in social contexts, such as classroom discussions or group problem-solving sessions.

🚀 Quick Start Guide (First 2-3 Weeks)

  1. Define Your Corpus: Decide what body of text you will analyze. Will it be research abstracts from a specific journal? A set of calculus textbooks? Transcripts of student interviews?
  2. Formulate a Research Question: What do you want to learn from this text? (e.g., "How has the use of formal vs. informal language in math research abstracts changed over the last 20 years?").
  3. Gather Your Data: Collect the texts for your corpus. This might involve downloading articles, scanning textbook pages, or transcribing audio.
  4. Choose Your Tools: Select the software you will use for your analysis. This will depend on whether your approach is more quantitative or qualitative.

🗓️ Suggested Timeline (12-14 Weeks)

  • Weeks 1-3
    Corpus Curation & Cleaning

    Gather all your texts and convert them into a consistent, clean format (e.g., plain text files). This is a critical and often time-consuming step.

  • Weeks 4-7
    Initial Analysis & Coding

    Perform the main analysis. For a quantitative approach, this means running frequency counts, topic models, or sentiment analysis. For a qualitative approach, it involves close reading and coding the discourse for specific features.

  • Weeks 8-10
    Pattern Identification & Interpretation

    Analyze the output from the previous step. What patterns, themes, or statistical trends emerge? What do these patterns mean in the context of your research question?

  • Weeks 11-14
    Writing the Report & Visualizing Results

    Write up your findings. Create visualizations (e.g., word clouds, frequency charts, network graphs) to help communicate your results effectively. Discuss the implications of your findings.

📄 Key Deliverables

Final Paper Structure
  • Introduction: Introduce the context and the body of text you are studying. State your research question.
  • Literature Review: Summarize previous research on language in mathematics or your specific topic.
  • Methods: Detail how you collected, cleaned, and analyzed your corpus. Justify your choice of analytical techniques.
  • Results: Present your findings, supported by data visualizations (for quantitative work) or illustrative examples from the text (for qualitative work).
  • Discussion: Interpret your findings. What do they reveal about mathematical communication? What are the limitations and implications of your study?
Recommended Tools
  • Corpus Analysis: AntConc (free, powerful tool for lexical analysis), Voyant Tools (web-based, great for visualization).
  • Programming: Python (with NLTK, spaCy, scikit-learn libraries) or R (with tidytext, quanteda packages) for more advanced, custom analyses.
  • Qualitative Analysis: NVivo, Dedoose, or Taguette for discourse analysis.
  • Writing: Overleaf (LaTeX) or a standard word processor.
