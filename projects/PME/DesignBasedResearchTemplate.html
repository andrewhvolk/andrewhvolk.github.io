🛠️ Design-Based Research Template

A Design-Based Research (DBR) project involves iteratively developing, testing, and refining educational tools, curricula, or games to enhance mathematical learning and engagement in a real-world setting.
← Back to All Project Types

Project Overview

DBR is a pragmatic methodology focused on creating and improving educational interventions. The research happens in cycles: you design something, test it in a real classroom, analyze the results, and then use those results to redesign and improve it for the next cycle. The goal is to produce both a usable educational product and a better understanding of how learning happens.

Common Methodologies:

  • Iterative Tool or Curriculum Design: The development and refinement of learning materials (like a software tool, a set of worksheets, or a new course module) through repeated cycles of implementation and analysis.
  • Digital Math Game Analysis: The creation or study of educational games to understand how game mechanics can be used to teach mathematical concepts and foster engagement.

🚀 Quick Start Guide (First 2-3 Weeks)

  1. Identify an Educational Problem: Pinpoint a specific learning challenge you want to solve (e.g., "Students have difficulty visualizing 3D calculus concepts.").
  2. Propose a Design Solution: Brainstorm an initial design for your intervention (e.g., "A simple interactive web app to rotate 3D surfaces.").
  3. Create a Low-Fidelity Prototype: Sketch out your design on paper or with a simple wireframing tool. This is your "Version 0.1."
  4. Plan Your First Cycle: Outline the plan for the first cycle of your research: How will you build the first version? How will you test it with students? What data will you collect? You will likely need IRB approval if testing with students.

🗓️ Suggested Timeline (14-16 Weeks)

  • Cycle 1 (Weeks 1-6)
    Design, Build & Test

    Develop the first functional version of your tool or curriculum. Conduct a small-scale test with a few students, collecting data through observations, think-aloud protocols, or short surveys.

  • Cycle 1 Analysis (Weeks 7-8)
    Analyze & Refine

    Analyze the data from your first test. What worked? What didn't? What bugs or usability issues did you find? Use these findings to create a list of revisions for the next version.

  • Cycle 2 (Weeks 9-12)
    Redesign, Rebuild & Re-test

    Implement the revisions to create "Version 2.0" of your intervention. Conduct a second, perhaps slightly larger, test to see if your changes improved the learning experience or outcomes.

  • Final Analysis & Report (Weeks 13-16)
    Synthesize & Write

    Analyze the data from your second cycle. Synthesize the findings from all cycles to tell the story of your design process. Write the final report, focusing on the "design principles" you learned.

📄 Key Deliverables

Final Paper Structure
  • Introduction: Describe the educational problem and the context of the learning environment. Introduce your initial design concept.
  • Methods: Describe the DBR methodology and the specific procedures for each research cycle (design, implementation, data collection, analysis).
  • Narrative of Cycles: Tell the story of your research, detailing the findings from each cycle and explaining how they informed the redesign for the next cycle.
  • Discussion & Design Principles: This is the most important section. Discuss what you learned about designing for learning in this context. Present a set of "design principles"—generalizable recommendations for others creating similar interventions.
Recommended Tools
  • Prototyping: Figma, Balsamiq (for wireframing); paper and pencil are great to start.
  • Development: Depending on the project, this could be HTML/CSS/JS for a web app, Python for a script, or a presentation tool for curricular materials.
  • Data Collection: Screen recording software (Loom, OBS), survey tools (Google Forms), audio recorders.
  • Writing: A standard word processor is usually fine.
