👁️ Visual & Multimodal Analysis Template

A visual analysis project uses visual data, recordings, and other multimodal artifacts to study mathematical thinking, communication, and interaction.
← Back to All Project Types

Project Overview

This type of research goes beyond text to analyze what can be seen and heard. It's used to understand the complex, real-time processes of doing mathematics, such as how students interpret diagrams, use gestures to explain concepts, or collaborate at a whiteboard. This approach requires specialized equipment and methods for capturing and analyzing rich visual data.

Common Methodologies:

  • Eye-Tracking or Gaze Path Analysis: Uses specialized hardware to track where a person is looking as they solve a problem or read a mathematical text with diagrams. This can reveal how students allocate their attention.
  • Video Ethnography of Problem Solving: Involves recording individuals or groups as they solve math problems and then analyzing the video for patterns in dialogue, gesture, and interaction with tools (like a whiteboard or calculator).

🚀 Quick Start Guide (First 2-3 Weeks)

  1. Identify a Visual Phenomenon: What visual aspect of mathematical practice interests you? (e.g., "How do students use diagrams when solving geometry problems?" or "What gestures do instructors use when explaining calculus concepts?").
  2. Formulate a Research Question: Frame a question that can be answered by analyzing visual data (e.g., "What are the common gaze patterns of experts versus novices when interpreting a complex graph?").
  3. Check Equipment Availability: These projects often require specialized equipment (like an eye-tracker) or a controlled lab setting. Confirm with your faculty advisor what resources are available.
  4. Develop a Data Collection Protocol: Plan the specific task participants will perform while being recorded. Draft your consent forms and submit your project for IRB approval.

🗓️ Suggested Timeline (12-15 Weeks)

  • Weeks 1-4
    Protocol Design, Pilot Testing & IRB

    Design your experimental task. Pilot test it to ensure it works as expected. Finalize your protocol and submit your IRB application.

  • Weeks 5-8
    Data Collection

    Once IRB approved, recruit your participants and run them through your protocol in the lab, collecting the eye-tracking or video data.

  • Weeks 9-11
    Data Processing & Analysis

    Process the raw data. For eye-tracking, this involves defining "areas of interest" and calculating metrics. For video, it involves transcribing speech and coding gestures or interactions using specialized software.

  • Weeks 12-15
    Interpretation & Final Report

    Analyze your coded data to find patterns. Write your paper, using visualizations like heat maps (for eye-tracking) or annotated screenshots (for video) as key evidence.

📄 Key Deliverables

Final Paper Structure
  • Introduction: Introduce the phenomenon you are studying and why a visual analysis is necessary. State your research question.
  • Methods: Detail your participants, the task they performed, the equipment used, and your specific procedure for coding and analyzing the visual data.
  • Results: Present your findings, supported by data visualizations (heat maps, gaze plots) and/or illustrative clips and transcripts from your video data.
  • Discussion: Interpret your findings. What do they tell us about mathematical cognition or communication? Discuss limitations and implications for future research or teaching practice.
Recommended Tools
  • Eye-Tracking: Tobii or SR Research hardware and their associated analysis software (often available in university psychology or human-computer interaction labs).
  • Video Analysis: ELAN or Ad-A-VANCE for detailed coding of video and audio streams.
  • General Tools: A good quality video camera and microphone.
  • Writing: Overleaf (LaTeX) or a standard word processor.
