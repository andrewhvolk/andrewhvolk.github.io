📚 Literature-Based Project Template

A literature-based project involves synthesizing and mapping published research to identify patterns, gaps, and consensus in a specific area of mathematics or math education.
← Back to All Project Types

Project Overview

This type of project is excellent for understanding the current state of a field. It does not require original data collection, making it a great starting point for undergraduate research. Your goal is to become an expert on what has already been studied on your topic and to present a clear, organized summary of the findings.

Common Methodologies:

  • Systematic Review: A structured, methodical synthesis of peer-reviewed literature on a focused research question. Often uses specific criteria (like the PRISMA guidelines) for including and excluding studies.
  • Scoping Review: A broader mapping of existing literature to identify key concepts, theories, sources of evidence, and gaps in the research. It answers the question, "What research has been done on this topic?"
  • Meta-Analysis: A quantitative approach that statistically combines the results of multiple scientific studies to derive a pooled estimate of effect. This is more advanced and requires a strong statistical background.

🚀 Quick Start Guide (First 1-2 Weeks)

  1. Choose a Topic: Pick a specific math or math education topic you are passionate about (e.g., "flipped classrooms in calculus," "math anxiety," "use of technology in geometry").
  2. Formulate Questions: Write 3-5 focused research questions. A good question is specific and answerable (e.g., "What are the documented effects of using inquiry-based learning on student performance in undergraduate proof-based courses?").
  3. Initial Search: Use databases like Google Scholar, MathSciNet, or your university's library portal. Try 5-10 different keyword combinations related to your topic.
  4. Screen and Select: Read the abstracts of at least 20-30 papers. Save the 10-15 most relevant ones to a reference manager like Zotero or Mendeley.

🗓️ Suggested Timeline (10-12 Weeks)

  • Weeks 1-2
    Topic Refinement & Protocol

    Finalize your research questions. Define your inclusion/exclusion criteria (e.g., only peer-reviewed articles from the last 10 years). Document your search strategy.

  • Weeks 3-5
    Comprehensive Literature Search

    Conduct an exhaustive search of all relevant databases. Screen titles and abstracts, then full texts, based on your criteria. Build your final library of articles.

  • Weeks 6-8
    Data Extraction & Synthesis

    Read each paper thoroughly. Extract key information (e.g., methodology, sample size, key findings, limitations) into a spreadsheet. Begin identifying common themes, patterns, and contradictions across the articles.

  • Weeks 9-10
    Drafting the Review

    Outline your paper (Introduction, Methods, Results, Discussion). Write the first draft, synthesizing the information you've gathered. Focus on telling a coherent story about the literature.

  • Weeks 11-12
    Revision & Finalization

    Revise your draft for clarity, flow, and accuracy. Check your citations and formatting. Get feedback from your faculty advisor and peers before submitting the final version.

📄 Key Deliverables

Final Paper Structure
  • Introduction: Background on the topic, statement of the problem, and your research questions.
  • Methods: Detailed description of your search strategy, databases used, keywords, and inclusion/exclusion criteria.
  • Results: A synthesis of the findings from the literature, organized by themes or categories. This is the main body of your paper.
  • Discussion: Summary of findings, interpretation of the results, limitations of the existing research, and suggestions for future research.
  • Conclusion: Brief wrap-up of the key takeaways.
  • References: A complete, consistently formatted list of all sources cited.
Recommended Tools
  • Reference Management: Zotero, Mendeley (essential for organizing sources).
  • Search Databases: Google Scholar, MathSciNet, JSTOR, ERIC, Academic Search Complete.
  • Writing & Typesetting: Microsoft Word, Google Docs, or Overleaf (for LaTeX).
  • Organization: A spreadsheet (Google Sheets, Excel) for data extraction.
