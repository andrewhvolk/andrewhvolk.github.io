🎤 Qualitative Inquiry Project Template

A qualitative project explores experiences, perceptions, and themes by gathering non-numerical data through methods like interviews, observations, and text analysis.
← Back to All Project Types

Project Overview

Qualitative research aims to understand the "why" and "how" behind a phenomenon. In math education, it can be used to explore student attitudes, teaching strategies, or the lived experience of learning a difficult concept. This approach values depth and context over broad generalization.

Common Methodologies:

  • Phenomenological Analysis: Seeks to understand the essence of a lived experience (e.g., "What is the experience of math anxiety for first-year college students?").
  • Grounded Theory: Aims to develop a theory that is "grounded" in the data itself. You start with a question, not a hypothesis, and build a theory from what your participants tell you.
  • Thematic Analysis: A flexible method for identifying, analyzing, and reporting patterns (themes) within qualitative data.
  • Case Study: An in-depth investigation of a single individual, group, classroom, or event to explore a real-life phenomenon in its context.

🚀 Quick Start Guide (First 2-3 Weeks)

  1. Identify Your Interest: What aspect of the human side of mathematics interests you? (e.g., student motivation, teacher beliefs, collaborative problem-solving).
  2. Draft Research Questions: Write open-ended questions that start with "how" or "what" (e.g., "How do undergraduate students describe their process for studying for a proof-based exam?").
  3. Design Your Protocol: Decide on your method (e.g., semi-structured interviews). Draft an interview guide with 8-10 open-ended questions.
  4. Submit for IRB Approval: Since you are working with human subjects, you **must** get approval from the Institutional Review Board (IRB) before you can recruit participants or collect data. This is a critical step.

🗓️ Suggested Timeline (12-14 Weeks)

  • Weeks 1-3
    Protocol Design & IRB Submission

    Finalize your research questions and interview protocol. Write and submit your IRB application. This can take several weeks to be approved.

  • Weeks 4-6
    Participant Recruitment & Data Collection

    Once you have IRB approval, begin recruiting participants. Conduct and audio-record your interviews or observations.

  • Weeks 7-8
    Transcription

    Transcribe your audio recordings into text. This is a time-consuming but essential step. You can do it manually or use transcription software.

  • Weeks 9-11
    Data Analysis & Coding

    Read through your transcripts multiple times. "Code" the data by highlighting key phrases and assigning labels. Group codes into broader themes to identify patterns.

  • Weeks 12-14
    Drafting, Revision & Finalization

    Outline and write your paper, using quotes from your data as evidence for your themes. Revise for clarity and get feedback from your advisor.

📄 Key Deliverables

Final Paper Structure
  • Introduction: Introduce the research problem and state your research questions.
  • Literature Review: Briefly summarize what is already known about your topic.
  • Methods: Detail your research design, participant selection, data collection process, and analysis method. Justify why your approach was appropriate.
  • Findings/Results: Present the key themes that emerged from your data. Use direct quotes from participants to support each theme.
  • Discussion: Interpret your findings. What do they mean? How do they relate to the existing literature? What are the limitations of your study and implications for the future?
Recommended Tools
  • Data Collection: A reliable digital audio recorder or smartphone app.
  • Transcription: Otter.ai (automated), oTranscribe (manual), or professional services.
  • Data Analysis: NVivo, Dedoose (paid); Taguette, QualCoder (free alternatives). A simple word processor or spreadsheet can also work for basic thematic analysis.
  • Writing & Typesetting: Microsoft Word, Google Docs, or Overleaf (for LaTeX).
