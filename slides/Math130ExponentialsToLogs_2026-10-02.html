Whiteboard Example 1: graph $f(x)=b^x$

Table, graph, asymptote

$f(x)=2^x$ and $g(x)=\left(\tfrac13\right)^x$

  1. $x=-2,-1,0,1,2$: $f$ gives $\tfrac14,\tfrac12,1,2,4$; $g$ gives $9,3,1,\tfrac13,\tfrac19$.
  2. Both pass through $(0,1)$ because $b^0=1$.
  3. Horizontal asymptote $y=0$. Domain $(-\infty,\infty)$, range $(0,\infty)$.
  4. $b>1$: growth (rises). $0<b<1$: decay (falls).

Model · 5 minFriday, Oct 2

6–11 minutes. Build both tables on the board, plot the points, and sketch the asymptote as a dashed line before drawing the curves. Ask: can 2^x ever equal zero or a negative number? That question justifies the asymptote and the range. Use the next slide to confirm the pattern.

[Sources] ALEKS 4.2 “Graphing an exponential function and its asymptote: f(x)=b^x” at 36% progress. New example written for this session.

Explore the base $b$

Every curve passes through $(0,1)$ and stays above $y=0$. What happens at $b=1$?

Explore · 1 minFriday, Oct 2

11–12 minutes. Slide the base below 1 and above 1. At b=1 the graph is the horizontal line y=1, which is why the base of an exponential function must satisfy b>0 and b≠1. The same restriction returns for logarithm bases later today.

Whiteboard Example 2: three interest models

$\$2000$ at $5\%$ for $6$ years

Find the balance under simple, quarterly, and continuous interest.

  1. Simple: $A=P(1+rt)=2000(1+0.05\cdot6)=\$2600.00$
  2. Quarterly: $A=P\left(1+\tfrac rn\right)^{nt}=2000\left(1+\tfrac{0.05}{4}\right)^{24}\approx\$2694.70$
  3. Continuous: $A=Pe^{rt}=2000e^{0.3}\approx\$2699.72$
  4. Simple interest $<$ compound $<$ continuous, for the same $P$, $r$, $t$.

Convert the rate to a decimal. Compute the exponent $nt$ or $rt$ first, then use parentheses.

Model · 6 minFriday, Oct 2

12–18 minutes. Write the three formulas side by side and label P, r, n, t in each. Ask students to supply n for quarterly (4) and the exponent nt=24 before calculating. Round only at the final step. The compound and continuous formulas are printed on Test 2; simple interest must be remembered.

Whiteboard Example 3: growth, decay, and models

(a) Decay by a percent

A $\$24{,}000$ car loses $15\%$ of its value each year. Find its value after $4$ years.

$V=24000(1-0.15)^4=24000(0.85)^4$

$\approx\$12{,}528.15$

(b) Evaluate a given model

$P(t)=18000(1.023)^t$ gives a town’s population $t$ years after 2020. Estimate the population in 2030.

$t=2030-2020=10$

$P(10)\approx22{,}596$ people

Growth factor $1+r$; decay factor $1-r$. Always find $t$ from the problem’s starting point.

Model · 4 minFriday, Oct 2

18–22 minutes. In (a), ask why the factor is 0.85 and not 0.15. In (b), the common error is substituting t=2030. Round the population to a whole number of people.

SECTION 4.3

A logarithm is an exponent

$\log_b x$ answers the question: “$b$ to what power gives $x$?”

Transition. Write the question form on the board and leave it up for the rest of class.

Whiteboard Example 4: convert between forms

$\log_b x = y \iff b^y = x$   ·   $\log x=\log_{10}x$   ·   $\ln x=\log_e x$

$\log_2 32=5$

$\iff 2^5=32$

$10^{-2}=0.01$

$\iff \log 0.01=-2$

$\ln x=3$

$\iff e^3=x$

$e^{0.4}=y$

$\iff \ln y=0.4$

Model · 5 minFriday, Oct 2

22–27 minutes. Teach the “base stays the base; the log equals the exponent” loop: circle the base, draw an arrow to the other side, then to the exponent. Spend the most time on the two natural-log rows, because ln conversion is the lowest topic in the class report (8%). The base of ln is e even though it is not written.

[Sources] ALEKS 4.3 “Converting between logarithmic and exponential equations” (23%) and “Converting between natural logarithmic and exponential equations” (8%). New examples written for this session.

Whiteboard Example 5: evaluate without a calculator

$\log_3 81$

$3^{?}=81 \Rightarrow \mathbf{4}$

$\log_5 \tfrac1{25}$

$5^{?}=5^{-2} \Rightarrow \mathbf{-2}$

$\log 1000$

$10^{?}=1000 \Rightarrow \mathbf{3}$

$\ln e^7$

$e^{?}=e^7 \Rightarrow \mathbf{7}$

$\log_4 2$

$4^{1/2}=2 \Rightarrow \mathbf{\tfrac12}$

$\log_9 1$

$9^{0}=1 \Rightarrow \mathbf{0}$

$\log_b x$ is defined only for $x>0$. So $\log_2(-8)$ and $\ln 0$ are undefined.

Model · 4 minFriday, Oct 2

27–31 minutes. Rewrite each as a question, “3 to what power is 81?”, before answering. The fraction and square-root cases are the ones students miss: rewrite 1/25 as 5^(−2) and 2 as 4^(1/2). Connect the domain warning to the range of b^x from Example 1.

[Sources] ALEKS 4.3 “Evaluating logarithmic expressions” at 18% progress. New examples written for this session.

Whiteboard Example 6: solve $\log_b a=c$

$\log_x 49=2$

$x^2=49 \Rightarrow x=7$  (reject $-7$)

$\log_2 x=-3$

$x=2^{-3}=\tfrac18$

$\ln x=2.5$

$x=e^{2.5}\approx12.1825$

$\log_3(2x-1)=2$

$2x-1=3^2=9 \Rightarrow x=5$

Convert to exponential form first. A base must be positive and not equal to $1$.

Model · 5 minFriday, Oct 2

31–36 minutes. The unknown can sit in the base, the argument, or inside an expression; the first step is always the conversion from Example 4. Ask why −7 is rejected as a base. For the last item, check that 2(5)−1=9 is positive.

[Sources] ALEKS 4.3 “Solving an equation of the form log_b a = c” at 21% progress. Inverse-log equations are marked Memorize in 130Test2.html. New examples written for this session.

Whiteboard Example 7: calculator and change of base

Round to four decimal places

$\log 250$,   $\ln 0.3$,   $\log_2 20$

  1. $\log 250\approx2.3979$  (use the LOG key)
  2. $\ln 0.3\approx-1.2040$  (negative because $0.3<1$)
  3. $\log_2 20=\dfrac{\ln 20}{\ln 2}\approx4.3219$
  4. Reasonable? $2^4=16$ and $2^5=32$, so the answer is between $4$ and $5$.

Model · 3 minFriday, Oct 2

36–39 minutes. Have students enter each value along with you. The estimate in the last step is the habit to build: it catches ln 2 / ln 20 inversions.

[Sources] ALEKS 4.3 “Using a calculator to evaluate natural and common logarithmic expressions” at 33%. Change of base is marked Memorize in 130Test2.html. New examples written for this session.

Whiteboard Example 8: solve exponential equations

(a)

$5^x=42$

$x=\log_5 42=\dfrac{\ln42}{\ln5}$

$x\approx2.3223$

(b)

$3e^{2x}=45$

$e^{2x}=15 \Rightarrow 2x=\ln15$

$x\approx1.3540$

(c) Back to Example 2

When does $\$2000$ at $5\%$ continuous reach $\$4000$?

$2000e^{0.05t}=4000$

$e^{0.05t}=2 \Rightarrow 0.05t=\ln2$

$t\approx13.86$ years

Isolate the exponential expression first. Then take $\ln$ of both sides (or convert to log form).

Model · 6 minFriday, Oct 2

39–45 minutes. In (b), the common error is taking ln before dividing by 3. Part (c) closes the loop with Section 4.2: logarithms answer “how long?” questions that exponential models raise.
