Rice on a Chessboard

In the old legend, a ruler offers a reward and the inventor of chess asks for rice: one grain on the first square, two on the second, four on the third, doubling on every square. It sounds modest. Pick any square to see how many grains it holds and how much rice is on the board so far.

Why the totals look the way they do

Grains on square n: 2n − 1
Total on squares 1 through n: 1 + 2 + 4 + … + 2n − 1 = 2n − 1

Square 1 has no doublings yet, so square n has n − 1 doublings. Each square holds one more grain than all the earlier squares together: square 4 holds 8 grains, and squares 1 to 3 hold 1 + 2 + 4 = 7. That is why the running total is always one less than the next power of 2.

The same idea gives the board's surprise: square 64 alone holds 263 grains, more than all 63 squares before it combined. Squares 1 through 32 together hold about 4.3 billion grains, roughly 100 tonnes; that is a big pile but a believable one. The second half of the board is where the numbers become impossible.
